AI chat tools can write code in seconds. So why learn to code at all? Because a tool that writes code is only useful to someone who **understands** the code. This lesson shows you how to use AI as a teacher, not as a ghost-writer.

## The gym analogy

Imagine you join a gym to get stronger. You could ask a friend to lift the weights for you. The weights would go up and down, and your exercise sheet would say "done". But your muscles would not change at all.

Learning to code works the same way. Your brain is the muscle. If an AI writes your assignment, the assignment is done, but **you did not get any stronger**. And in a job interview, or on your first day at work, the AI friend is not lifting for you.

## What AI is good for (while you learn)

Use AI like a patient tutor who never gets tired of your questions. Good uses:

- **Explaining a concept** in different words: "Explain what an HTML attribute is, like I am new to coding."
- **Explaining an error message** you got yourself: "What does this error mean in general?"
- **Explaining code you already wrote:** "Here is my HTML. Can you explain what each line does?"
- **Asking 'what if' questions:** "What happens if I forget to close a `<p>` tag?"
- **Making practice questions:** "Give me 5 quiz questions about HTML lists."

## What AI is not good for (while you learn)

Avoid these, especially in the first months:

- Asking it to **write your assignment** or any part of it.
- Pasting your broken code and asking it to "fix it" **before you have tried** the get-unstuck routine yourself.
- Using code you **cannot explain** line by line.
- Asking it to write your **reflections** or your introduction. We want to hear **your** voice, even if your English is not perfect.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** AI tools are sometimes wrong, and they sound confident even when they are wrong. They may use old methods or invent things that do not exist. Always check important facts against a trusted source like MDN.

## The "delete test"

Here is a simple test for any code you submit:

> If I deleted this and had to write it again from scratch, alone, could I do it?

If the answer is no, you have not learned it yet, even if the code works. Go back, understand it, and rebuild it yourself.

## Ask for understanding, not answers

The way you ask changes what you learn. Compare these:

| Not helpful for learning | Helpful for learning |
| --- | --- |
| "Write an About Me page in HTML." | "What HTML tags are usually used for a page with a heading, some paragraphs and a list?" |
| "Fix my code." | "My image is not showing. Here is my code. Can you give me a hint about where to look, without giving the full answer?" |
| "Write a form with a name and email." | "What is the difference between `type="text"` and `type="email"` on an input?" |

> 💡 **Tip:** You can tell the AI how to help you. For example, start with: "I am a beginner learning HTML. Please give me hints and ask me questions instead of giving me the full answer."

### Try it

Choose one of these words: **tag**, **browser**, or **file path**. Ask an AI tool (or a search engine, if you prefer) to explain it in simple English with an everyday analogy. Then write your own one-sentence explanation in your notes, **without** copying. If you cannot, ask a follow-up question.

## Don't ask the same thing twice

Learning means you need help **less** over time. Watch out for this pattern:

- Monday: "Write me a list of my favourite foods in HTML."
- Tuesday: "Write me a list of my favourite films in HTML."

You learned nothing on Monday, so you needed help again on Tuesday. Instead, on Monday ask **how** lists work, then write the food list yourself. On Tuesday you will not need help at all.

## Be honest

If you used AI to help you understand something for an assignment, that is fine. Just say so in your submission, for example: "I asked an AI to explain what `alt` text is, then I wrote the page myself." Honesty helps your instructor understand where you are, and give you better feedback.

> 🧠 **Remember:** Your instructor is not marking you for perfect code. They want to see **your** thinking, including your mistakes. Mistakes show us what to teach next.
