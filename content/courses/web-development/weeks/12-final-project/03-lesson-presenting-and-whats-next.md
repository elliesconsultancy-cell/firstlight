Think about a friend who tells a story at a family party. A good storyteller has a clear start, a middle and an end. People listen and remember.

A demo is a short story about your project. Building something is one skill. Explaining it is another. In a job interview, explaining is often the skill that matters most. In this lesson you learn a simple structure for a five-minute demo. Then we look at where you can go from here.

## Why demos matter

People in tech jobs present their work all the time: to their team, to managers, to customers. In interviews you will often hear, "Tell me about a project you built."

A clear, calm answer shows that you understand your own code and can explain it. Employers look for exactly this.

A demo is a skill, not a talent. With a simple structure and some practice, anyone can do it well.

## A five-minute demo structure

Tell a short story: a problem, a hero (your app) and a happy ending. There are five parts.

### 1. The problem (30 seconds)

Who is this for? What problem does it solve?

> "My mum keeps her recipes on scraps of paper all over the kitchen. She can never find the quick ones on a busy weekday."

### 2. The live demo (2 minutes)

Show the app working. Follow one real user's journey. Do not explain every button. Tell the story.

> "Let's say it's Tuesday and I've only got 20 minutes. I tick 'under 30 minutes'... and here are three recipes. I click on beans on toast, and I can see the ingredients."

### 3. Under the hood (1 minute)

Show **one** piece of code you are proud of. Explain it in plain words.

> "All the recipes are in an array of objects. When I tick the filter, I update the state and call my render function, which draws the cards again."

### 4. Challenges and learning (1 minute)

Share one thing that was hard and how you solved it. This shows how you think.

> "My filters didn't work together at first: picking 'vegetarian' cancelled 'quick'. I fixed it by applying both filters one after the other to the same array."

### 5. What's next (30 seconds)

What would you add with more time? Then say thank you and invite questions.

> "Next, I'd like to save recipes with localStorage. Thanks for listening, I'm happy to take questions!"

## Preparing for the demo

- **Practise out loud** at least twice, with a timer. Five minutes goes faster than you think.
- **Use the live site**, not your local files. Open it in a browser tab before you start.
- **Prepare your data.** Have good example items ready. Do not type "test test" in front of everyone.
- **Zoom in.** Press `Ctrl` and `+` (or `Cmd` and `+` on a Mac), so people can read the screen.
- **Close other tabs** and turn off notifications.
- **Have a backup.** Take screenshots or a short screen recording, in case the internet fails.

> 💡 **Tip:** If something breaks during your demo, stay calm and say what happened: "Ah, that's a bug I haven't seen before. I'll make a note to fix it." This happens to professionals too. Staying calm makes a good impression.

## Answering questions

You will get questions. You do not need to know everything.

- If you know the answer, keep it short and plain.
- If you do not know, it is fine to say: "Good question. I'm not sure yet. I'd find out by checking MDN, or by trying it in the console." This is an honest and professional answer.
- If a question is really a suggestion, say thank you and write it down.

## Try it

Write a short outline for your own demo using the five parts above. Write one or two sentences for each part.

Then record yourself with your phone or a screen recorder. Watch it back. Note one thing you did well and one thing to improve.

## What's next?

Finishing this course is a real achievement. Here are some paths you can take. You do not need to do all of them. Pick one direction and keep going steadily.

### Build your portfolio

A **portfolio** is a simple website that shows who you are and what you have built. You already have the skills to make one with HTML, CSS and GitHub Pages.

- An "About me" section: who you are, what you are learning, what you are looking for.
- Three to five of your best projects. Give each one a screenshot, a short description, a live link and a repo link.
- Contact details: email, LinkedIn and GitHub.

Quality beats quantity. Three polished projects with good READMEs are better than ten unfinished ones.

### Keep learning

Some good next topics, roughly in order:

- **More JavaScript:** modules, classes, and working with `localStorage`.
- **React:** the most popular library for building user interfaces. You already know its core idea: state and render. The official tutorial is at react.dev.
- **Node.js and back end:** building your own APIs and working with databases.
- **Testing:** go further with Jest, which you met in week 9.
- **Accessibility:** making sure everyone can use what you build. Employers value this highly.

Free resources that are worth your time:

- **MDN Web Docs** (developer.mozilla.org) – the reference for HTML, CSS and JavaScript.
- **javascript.info** – a detailed, free JavaScript book.
- **freeCodeCamp** (freecodecamp.org) – free courses and certifications with projects.
- **The Odin Project** (theodinproject.com) – a free full-stack path built around projects.
- **Full Stack Open** (fullstackopen.com) – a free university course on React, Node and more.

### Move towards a job

- **Code a little, often.** Thirty minutes a day beats five hours once a month.
- **Keep your GitHub active.** Small, regular commits show that you are still learning.
- **Join communities.** Local meetups, online groups and volunteer projects are good places to learn and to hear about opportunities.
- **Learn in public.** Share what you are building on LinkedIn. Short posts like "This week I learned how to use fetch" help people notice you.
- **Practise explaining your projects.** Your demo practice is interview practice.

> 🧠 **Remember:** Everyone's path into tech is different. Many people come from completely different careers. You are not "behind". Keep going.

## Instructor agenda

### Learning objectives
- Learners can present their project with a clear five-part structure.
- Learners can answer questions honestly, including "I do not know yet".
- Learners can choose their next steps for learning and for a portfolio.

### Purpose
Explaining a project is a job skill, because interviewers often say "tell me about a project you built". This lesson also helps learners leave with a direction.

### Things to teach
1. **The five parts.** Problem, live demo, under the hood, challenges and learning, what is next. Use the recipe box examples. Tell it as a story.
2. **Prepare.** Practise out loud, use the live site, zoom in and close other tabs. Have good example data and a backup such as screenshots or a recording.
3. **When something goes wrong.** Stay calm and say what happened. Use the lesson's "that is a bug I have not seen before" example.
4. **Questions.** Keep answers short. "Good question, I would find out with MDN or the console" is a good, honest answer.
5. **What is next.** Portfolio with three polished projects, then React, Node or testing. Keep your GitHub active and learn in public.

### Check understanding
- Ask: "What goes in 'under the hood'?" A good answer: one piece of code you are proud of, explained in plain words.
- Ask: "A question stumps you. What do you say?" A good answer: be honest, and say how you would find out.
- Ask: "Why use the live site in a demo?" A good answer: it shows the real published app, not local files.

### Watch for
- Learners who explain every button. Remind them to follow one user's journey.
- Learners who read from the screen. Ask them to practise with a friend first.
