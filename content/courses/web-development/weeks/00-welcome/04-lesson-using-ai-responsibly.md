AI chat tools can write code in seconds. So why learn to code? Because code is only useful to someone who **understands** it. In this lesson you learn to use AI as a teacher, not as a ghost-writer (someone who does your work and lets you take the credit).

## The gym picture

Imagine you join a gym to get stronger. You ask a friend to lift the weights for you. The weights go up and down. Your sheet says "done". But your muscles do not change at all.

Learning to code is the same. Your brain is the muscle. If an AI writes your assignment, the assignment is done, but **you did not get stronger**. On your first day at work, the AI friend will not be lifting for you.

## What AI is good for

Use AI like a patient tutor who never gets tired of your questions. Good uses:

- **Explain an idea in other words.** "Explain what an HTML attribute is, like I am new to coding."
- **Explain an error you got.** "What does this error mean in general?"
- **Explain code you already wrote.** "Here is my HTML. What does each line do?"
- **Ask "what if" questions.** "What happens if I forget to close a `<p>` tag?"
- **Make practice questions.** "Give me 5 quiz questions about HTML lists."

## What AI is not good for

Avoid these, especially in your first months:

- Asking it to **write your assignment**, or any part of it.
- Pasting broken code and asking "fix it" **before you tried** the get-unstuck routine.
- Using code you **cannot explain** line by line.
- Asking it to write your **introduction or reflections**. We want **your** voice, even if your English is not perfect.

> ⚠️ **Watch out:** AI tools are sometimes wrong, and they sound sure even then. They may use old methods or invent things that do not exist. Check important facts in a trusted source like MDN.

## The "delete test"

Use this test on any code you submit:

> If I deleted this and had to write it again from scratch, alone, could I do it?

If the answer is no, you have not learned it yet, even if the code works. Go back, understand it, and build it again yourself.

## Ask for understanding, not answers

How you ask changes what you learn. Compare:

| Not helpful for learning | Helpful for learning |
| --- | --- |
| "Write an About Me page in HTML." | "What HTML tags are usually used for a page with a heading, some paragraphs and a list?" |
| "Fix my code." | "My image is not showing. Here is my code. Can you give me a hint about where to look, without the full answer?" |
| "Write a form with a name and email." | "What is the difference between `type="text"` and `type="email"` on an input?" |

> 💡 **Tip:** You can tell the AI how to help you. Start with: "I am a beginner learning HTML. Please give me hints and ask me questions instead of giving me the full answer."

### Try it

Choose one word: **tag**, **browser**, or **file path**. Ask an AI tool (or a search engine) to explain it in simple English, with an everyday picture. Then write your own one-sentence explanation in your notes. Do not copy. If you cannot, ask a follow-up question.

## Do not ask the same thing twice

Learning means you need help **less** over time. Watch out for this pattern:

- Monday: "Write me a list of my favourite foods in HTML."
- Tuesday: "Write me a list of my favourite films in HTML."

You learned nothing on Monday, so you needed help again on Tuesday. Do this instead. On Monday, ask **how** lists work. Then write the food list yourself. On Tuesday you will not need help.

## Be honest

If AI helped you understand something for an assignment, that is fine. Say so in your submission. For example: "I asked an AI to explain what `alt` text is. Then I wrote the page myself."

Honesty helps your instructor see where you are. Then they can give better feedback.

> 🧠 **Remember:** Your instructor is not looking for perfect code. They want to see **your** thinking, including your mistakes. Mistakes show us what to teach next.

## Check your understanding

2. Give two good ways to use AI while learning.
3. What is the "delete test"?
4. Why should you check AI answers in a trusted source?

## Instructor agenda

### Learning objectives
- By the end, learners can describe good and bad uses of AI while learning
- apply the delete test to code they submit
- ask AI for understanding and hints, not answers
- say honestly in a submission when AI helped

### Purpose
Learners will use AI whether or not we allow it. If they learn to use it as a tutor, they build real skill. If it writes their work, they leave the course unable to code.

### Things to teach
1. **The gym picture.** If a friend lifts the weights, your muscles do not grow. The brain is the muscle. Ask learners what happens on day one at a job.
2. **Good uses and bad uses.** Read the two lists together. Good: explain an idea, an error, or code you wrote. Bad: write the assignment, fix my code before you tried, code you cannot explain.
3. **The delete test.** If you deleted it and had to write it again alone, could you? If not, you have not learned it yet.
4. **Ask for understanding.** Use the lesson's table. Have learners rewrite "Write an About Me page in HTML" into a better question. Show the tip: ask for hints and questions, not full answers.
5. **Be honest.** Show the example sentence about asking AI to explain `alt` text. Say that honesty lets you give better feedback, and mistakes help you know what to teach.

### Check understanding
- Ask: "What is the delete test?" A good answer: could I rewrite this alone from scratch? If not, I have not learned it.
- Ask: "Why check AI answers on MDN?" A good answer: AI can sound sure and still be wrong or out of date.
- Ask: "Give a good prompt and a bad one." A good answer: "What tags suit a page with a list?" is good; "Write my page" is bad.

### Watch for
- Learners who feel judged and hide their AI use. Say clearly that honest use is welcome.
- Learners who use AI for the introduction in this week's assignment. Remind them we want their own voice.
